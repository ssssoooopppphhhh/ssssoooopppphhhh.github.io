![image](/assets/images/250526_geomFlower.png)

<p>In the last five years, a major focus of my research has been on the regulation of small, membrane-associated GTPases in the Ras superfamily. Before that, I worked in the antibody discovery world. Naturally, I was stoked to stumble upon a paper discussing the development of antibody tools to interrogate the activation of K-Ras GTPase and wrote up this summary highlighting the aspects of the paper that most stood out to me:</p>

<p>Reference: Fischer, B., Uchański, T., Sheryazdanova, A., Gonzalez, S., Volkov, A. N., Brosens, E., ... & Steyaert, J. (2024). Allosteric nanobodies to study the interactions between SOS1 and RAS. Nature Communications, 15(1), 6214.
</p>

<p>Dysregulation of the small GTPase K-Ras is a major driver of human cancers. The active, GTP-bound state of K-Ras is promoted by the guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) SOS1. Although the interaction between K-Ras and SOS1 presents a logical therapeutic target, short-lived protein-protein interactions (PPIs) like that of K-Ras and SOS1 remain challenging to study and modulate. In a recent article, Fischer et al. establish a novel platform for generating highly specific nanobodies that either stabilize or disrupt PPIs. Using this approach, they identify four distinct nanobodies that modulate the K-Ras-SOS1 interaction.
</p>

<p>Due to their small size and high specificity, nanobodies have emerged as valuable tools for both therapeutic and research applications. To generate nanobodies against the K-Ras-SOS1 heterodimer, the authors chemically crosslinked the complex to create a stable antigen for llama immunization. They then constructed a yeast display nanobody library and used multicolor fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) to enrich for clones that bind K-Ras, SOS1, or the K-Ras-SOS1 heterodimer.
</p>

<p>Their screening pipeline yielded four nanobodies, Nb77, Nb84, Nb14, and Nb22, for which they obtained high-resolution crystal structures. Nb77 binds the catalytic domain of SOS1, preventing it from interacting with K-Ras and inhibiting nucleotide exchange. Conversely, Nb84 targets the SOS1 binding site on K-Ras, blocking SOS1-mediated nucleotide exchange and stabilizing K-Ras in an inactive conformation. Both Nb77 and Nb84 attenuate K-Ras activation in a dose-dependent manner.
</p>

<p>Intriguingly, the remaining nanobodies, Nb14 and Nb22, stabilize the K-Ras-SOS1 complex and enhance K-Ras activation through distinct mechanisms. Using a stopped-flow assay, the authors compared the kinetics of K-Ras-SOS1 heterodimerization and nucleotide exchange in the presence of these nanobodies as well as a previously characterized K-Ras mutant, K-Ras<sup>Y64A</sup>. K-Ras<sup>Y64A</sup> is known to allosterically promote binding of SOS1 to K-Ras while itself lacking affinity for the SOS1 catalytic domain. The authors find that Nb14, which binds at the K-Ras-SOS1 interface, and K-Ras<sup>Y64A</sup> both increase the nucleotide release rate. These results challenge the prevailing model of K-Ras activation, which proposes that allosteric regulation accelerates K-Ras-SOS1 complex formation rather than nucleotide exchange. By contrast, structural analysis revealed that the contacts between Nb22 and the K-Ras-SOS1 heterodimer are located almost entirely on SOS1. Unlike Nb14 and K-Ras<sup>Y64A</sup>, Nb22 does increase the rate of K-Ras-SOS1 heterodimerization without affecting the rate of nucleotide release.
</p>

<p>Fischer et al. introduce four nanobodies that provide new tools for tuning the K-Ras-SOS1 interaction as well as downstream signaling pathways. More broadly, their study establishes a workflow for generating nanobodies that selectively target transient PPIs. With additional experimentation, this work may expose novel therapeutic strategies.
</p>
